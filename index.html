A DATA STORY - AUSTRALIA - 2006-2025

Everyday Life in Australia

Much of the work that holds Australian communities together is never paid. People coach junior sport, check on a neighbour, drive a parent to the doctor, and care for others. This story explores how Australians give their time through volunteering, caring, and staying active.

PART 1 - EVERYDAY CONTRIBUTION

Fewer people volunteer, but millions still give their time

Australians contribute to their communities through formal volunteering, but participation has fallen and differs from place to place.

In 2010, more than one in three Australians volunteered through an organisation. By 2025, this had fallen to fewer than one in four. The fall began before COVID-19 and has not recovered since.

Formal volunteering remains an important part of everyday community life, even as the way Australians give their time continues to change.

Why this matters: organised volunteering is only one part of everyday contribution. The next chart looks at informal help outside organisations.

How many people help and how common is it?

Everyday help includes support given outside an organisation. Looking at both numbers and percentages helps us understand how this support varies across Australia.

Larger circles mean more people helping. States with larger populations can have more people providing help, even when a smaller percentage of their residents participates.

Darker orange means a higher percentage helping. This allows us to compare participation across states and territories with different population sizes.

The percentage ranges from 27.8% in Tasmania to 38.0% in the Northern Territory a difference of 10.2 percentage points.

Why this matters: A small circle does not mean helping is uncommon. Read its colour alongside its size to understand both the scale of support and participation.

All states recorded a decline

Every line slopes downward between 2020 and 2025. This means formal volunteering became less common in every Australian state and territory.

Australian Capital Territory is highlighted in orange. It recorded the largest fall: 8.9 percentage points, from 30.9% to 22.0%.

Tasmania remained the highest in 2025 at 27.5%, while Victoria had the lowest rate at 21.8%.

Why this matters: The decline is nationwide rather than limited to one location, suggesting a broader change in participation in organised community life.

PART 2 - WHERE UNPAID CARERS LIVE

Care keeps everyday life moving across Australia

Unpaid carers support family members, friends and neighbours with disability, long-term illness or old age. Their care is part of everyday life in households and communities.

Melbourne has the largest estimated number of unpaid carers (505,817), followed by Sydney (454,470).

Each dot represents 5,000 carers. Because this map shows total numbers, larger clusters mostly appear in more populated places.

What this does not show: More dots do not necessarily mean unpaid care is more common there. The next chart compares the percentage of residents providing care, which is the normalised measure.

CHILDCARE FOLLOWS A DIFFERENT PATTERN

Where is unpaid childcare highest?

Share of people aged 15+ who cared for a child without pay in the two weeks before Census night. Squares represent capital cities.

Takeaway: The Northern Territory stands out: 36.2% of people outside Darwin provided unpaid childcare, compared with roughly one quarter in most other regions. Perth has the highest share among capital cities at 29.1%.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Census 2021 QuickStats, provided unpaid care for own or other children.

Childcare follows a different pattern

Around 5.5 million people, or about one in four Australians aged 15 and over, provided unpaid care for a child in the two weeks before the 2021 Census.

The geographic pattern is clearest in the Northern Territory, where unpaid childcare is most common outside Darwin.

Why this matters: Outside Darwin, the Northern Territory's childcare rate is 9.2 percentage points higher than Darwin's (36.2% versus 27.0%). This suggests unpaid childcare may play a particularly important role in regional and remote communities, where access to formal childcare can differ. The map identifies this gap, but does not explain its cause.

Who carries unpaid caring work?

Share of women and men in each age group who provided unpaid care. The line between dots shows the gender gap.

Takeaway: Women are more likely than men to provide unpaid care from their 30s through their 70s, peaking at 23.1% among women aged 55-59. From age 80, men are more likely to provide care.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Census 2021, unpaid assistance to a person with disability, a long-term health condition or old age, by age and sex.

The busiest years for caring

Unpaid caring rises through working life and peaks in the late 50s. At this point, 23.1% of women provide care, compared with 14.8% of men.

The gender gap is small among younger adults, then widens from the 30s and is largest around ages 50-59. This is a stage of life when many people may be combining paid work with care for parents, partners, relatives or other people close to them.

The pattern changes among older Australians. From age 80, men are more likely than women to provide unpaid care, showing that caring responsibilities do not follow one fixed pattern across the life course.

Why this matters: Women in mid-to-later adulthood are especially likely to carry unpaid care while also being in working age. Flexible leave, respite services and accessible local support can help reduce the pressure on people balancing care with employment and family responsibilities.

How do three types of contribution differ?

Each number is a state or territory's rate. Colour shows how far it sits above or below the Australian rate for that contribution type.

Takeaway: The Northern Territory's biggest lead is in informal volunteering: 38.0%, or 8.5 points above the Australian rate of 29.5%.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6.3. Australian rates: formal volunteering 22.6%, informal volunteering 29.5%, unpaid help to others 45.0%.

High in one, not always in all

Putting the three ways of giving side by side shows that places have different habits. The Northern Territory is above the national rate on all three measures, while New South Wales is below on all three.

Tasmania leads formal volunteering at 27.5%, but its informal volunteering rate is 1.7 points below the Australian rate. In contrast, Victoria and Western Australia are near the national rate for formal volunteering while recording higher rates of unpaid help to others.

Why this matters: A single volunteering measure can miss important unpaid support happening outside organisations. Looking across all three measures gives a fuller picture of how communities contribute and helps governments or local organisations decide whether formal volunteering programs, informal support networks, or both need attention.

PART 3 - RECREATION AND TIME

Most of us stay active, mostly on our own terms

Australians use their free time in different ways, from a daily walk to Saturday club sport.

Being active is close to universal: 85% of adults did some sport or physical activity in 2025. Around seven in ten are active every week, but only about one in five every day.

People aged 55-64 are the most regular. They rank first from once a week up to five times a week, leading the next age group by 1 to 4 percentage points. Teenagers aged 15-17 climb to first for daily activity (24.3%).

People aged 65 and over rank last at most steps, but from four to six times a week they move ahead of adults aged 35-44.

Why this matters: Free time is shared with everything else in this story. The same hours can go to a club, a carer's role or a walk around the block.
