A DATA STORY - AUSTRALIA - 2006-2025

Everyday Life in Australia

Much of the work that holds Australian communities together is never paid. People coach junior sport, check on a neighbour, drive a parent to the doctor, and care for others. This story explores how Australians give their time through volunteering, caring, and staying active.

PART 1 - EVERYDAY CONTRIBUTION

Fewer people volunteer, but millions still give their time

Australians contribute to their communities through formal volunteering, but participation has fallen and differs from place to place.

In 2010, more than one in three Australians volunteered through an organisation. By 2025, this had fallen to fewer than one in four. The fall began before COVID-19 and has not recovered since.

Formal volunteering remains an important part of everyday community life, even as the way Australians give their time continues to change.

Why this matters: organised volunteering is only one part of everyday contribution. The next chart looks at informal help outside organisations.

How many people help and how common is it?

Everyday help includes support given outside an organisation. Looking at both numbers and percentages helps us understand how this support varies across Australia.

Larger circles mean more people helping. States with larger populations can have more people providing help, even when a smaller percentage of their residents participates.

Darker orange means a higher percentage helping. This allows us to compare participation across states and territories with different population sizes.

The percentage ranges from 27.8% in Tasmania to 38.0% in the Northern Territory a difference of 10.2 percentage points.

Why this matters: A small circle does not mean helping is uncommon. Read its colour alongside its size to understand both the scale of support and participation.

All states recorded a decline

Every line slopes downward between 2020 and 2025. This means formal volunteering became less common in every Australian state and territory.

The ACT is highlighted in orange. It recorded the largest fall: 8.9 percentage points, from 30.9% to 22.0%.

Tasmania remained the highest in 2025 at 27.5%, while Victoria had the lowest rate at 21.8%.

Why this matters: The decline is nationwide rather than limited to one location, suggesting a broader change in participation in organised community life.
