A DATA STORY - AUSTRALIA - 2006-2025

Everyday Life in Australia

Much of the work that holds Australian communities together is never paid. People coach junior sport, check on a neighbour, drive a parent to the doctor, and care for others. This story explores how Australians give their time through volunteering, caring, and staying active.

PART 1 - EVERYDAY CONTRIBUTION

Fewer people volunteer, but millions still give their time

Australians contribute to their communities through formal volunteering, but participation has fallen and differs from place to place.

In 2010, more than one in three Australians volunteered through an organisation. By 2025, this had fallen to fewer than one in four. The fall began before COVID-19 and has not recovered since.

Even so, about 5 million Australians still volunteered through an organisation in 2025. Together they gave 618 million hours, up from 490 million in 2020.

The most common reasons for not volunteering are lack of time (47%) and lack of interest (36%).

Volunteering peaks among people aged 45 to 54 (28.0%) and is lowest among 25 to 34 year olds (13.1%).

Why this matters: organised volunteering is only one part of everyday contribution. The next chart looks at informal help outside organisations.

How many people help and how common is it?

Everyday help, or informal volunteering, is unpaid support given to people outside your household, other than family, and not through an organisation.

New South Wales has the most people helping, about 2.0 million. Yet its rate of 28.5% is among the lowest, level with the ACT.

The Northern Territory is the reverse: the highest rate, but only about 64,000 people, around one-thirtieth of the NSW total.

The rate ranges from 27.8% in Tasmania to 38.0% in the Northern Territory, a difference of 10.2 percentage points.

Informal help is more common than organised volunteering in every state and territory. Nationally, 29.5% helped informally in just four weeks, against 22.6% who volunteered through an organisation over a whole year.

Since 2020, informal help has fallen in six of the eight states and territories. Only South Australia (28.9% to 29.3%) and the Northern Territory (36.2% to 38.0%) rose.

Why this matters: A small circle does not mean helping is uncommon. Planning by population size alone would miss the places where helping is most common.

Where is organised volunteering most common?

Organised volunteering is unpaid work done through an organisation, such as a charity, sports club, school or community group.

The smallest places volunteer at the highest rates. Tasmania (27.5%) and the Northern Territory (26.7%) lead, about 4 to 5 points above the Australian rate of 22.6%.

The three largest states, New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland, all sit below that rate. Because they hold most of the population, they pull the national figure down towards their own.

A high rate is not the same as a large number. Tasmania's 27.5% equals about 129,000 volunteers. New South Wales, at 22.3%, has about 1.55 million, roughly twelve times as many.

Volunteering also rises with distance from the cities: 20.9% in major cities, 26.7% in inner regional areas, 28.0% in outer regional areas and 34.0% in remote areas.

Victoria has the lowest rate (21.8%), yet it has the second-largest number of formal volunteers, about 1.25 million.

Why this matters: Differences between places show that communities may need different approaches to support and encourage organised volunteering.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, General Social Survey 2020 and 2025, Table 6.3, via Volunteering Australia, Volunteering Statistics: GSS 2025 (May 2026), Table 6.

All states recorded a decline

The ACT's fall of 8.9 points took it from first place in 2020 to equal sixth in 2025, level with Queensland.

Elsewhere, falls ranged from 0.8 points in New South Wales (23.1% to 22.3%) to 3.4 points in Queensland.

Because the leaders fell further, states became more alike. The gap between highest and lowest shrank from 7.8 points in 2020 to 5.7 points in 2025, now Tasmania (27.5%) to Victoria (21.8%).

The ACT's fall went beyond organised volunteering. Its informal volunteering rate also dropped, from 38.9% in 2020 to 28.5% in 2025, the largest fall of any state or territory.

As the ACT fell, Tasmania and the Northern Territory moved up to first and second. New South Wales rose from last place to fifth, simply by falling least.

Nationally, the rate went from 25% in 2020 to 22.6% in 2025. The ABS does not rate this national change as statistically significant.

Why this matters: The decline is nationwide rather than limited to one location, suggesting a broader change in participation in organised community life.

PART 2 - WHERE UNPAID CARERS LIVE

Care keeps everyday life moving across Australia

Unpaid carers support family members, friends and neighbours with disability, long-term illness or old age. Their care is part of everyday life in households and communities.

Melbourne has the most unpaid carers (505,817), more than Sydney (454,470) despite its smaller population.

As a share of residents, 12.6% of Melburnians provide care, against 10.6% in Sydney.

Together, Sydney and Melbourne are home to about 960,000 carers, close to two in five of the national total.

What this does not show: More dots do not necessarily mean care is more common. By share of residents, the rest of Victoria is highest (14.1%) and Darwin is lowest (9.0%).

Childcare follows a different pattern

Around 5.5 million people, or about one in four Australians aged 15 and over, provided unpaid care for a child in the two weeks before the 2021 Census.

Outside the Northern Territory, capital cities usually rate higher. In five of the six other states, the capital is above the rest of the state. New South Wales is the exception.

Elsewhere, every region sits between 24.3% (rest of South Australia) and 29.1% (Perth), a range of just 4.8 points.

Rates and numbers differ here too. Sydney has the lowest capital rate (25.2%), yet more than 1 million people there gave unpaid childcare.

The widest gap outside the Territory is in South Australia: Adelaide (27.1%) against 24.3% in the rest of the state.

Why this matters: Outside Darwin, the Northern Territory's childcare rate is 9.2 percentage points higher than Darwin's (36.2% versus 27.0%). This suggests unpaid childcare may play a particularly important role in regional and remote communities, where access to formal childcare can differ. The map identifies this gap, but does not explain its cause.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Census 2021, unpaid assistance to a person with disability, a long-term health condition or old age, by age and sex.

The busiest years for caring

Unpaid caring rises through working life and peaks in the late 50s. At this point, 23.1% of women provide care, compared with 14.8% of men.

The gender gap is small among younger adults, then widens from the 30s and is largest at ages 55-59 (8.3 points). This is a stage of life when many people may be combining paid work with care for parents, partners, relatives or other people close to them.

The pattern reverses among the oldest Australians. From age 80, men are more likely than women to provide care, and at 85 and over they are more than twice as likely (9.7% against 4.6%).

Women's rate more than quadruples, from 5.2% at ages 15-19 to 23.1% at 55-59. Men's rate is steadier, staying between 11% and 15% from their mid-40s to their early 80s.

Men's caring also peaks at ages 55-59 (14.8%), the same stage of life as women's.

Why this matters: Women in mid-to-later adulthood are especially likely to carry unpaid care while also of working age. Flexible leave, respite services and accessible local support can help reduce the pressure on people balancing care with employment and family responsibilities.

High in one, not always in all

Putting the three ways of giving side by side shows that places have different habits. The Northern Territory is above the national rate on all three measures, while New South Wales is below on all three.

Tasmania leads formal volunteering at 27.5%, but its informal volunteering rate is 1.7 points below the Australian rate. In contrast, Victoria and Western Australia are near the national rate for formal volunteering while recording higher rates of unpaid help to others.

Unpaid help to others is the most common form of giving everywhere, from 42.6% in New South Wales to 49.0% in the Northern Territory.

Tasmania is the only place where formal and informal volunteering are almost equal (27.5% and 27.8%). Elsewhere, informal help leads by at least 5.7 points.

Why this matters: A single volunteering measure can miss important unpaid support happening outside organisations. Looking across all three measures gives a fuller picture of how communities contribute and helps governments or local organisations decide whether formal volunteering programs, informal support networks, or both need attention.

PART 3 - RECREATION AND TIME

Most of us stay active, mostly on our own terms

Australians use their free time in different ways, from a daily walk to Saturday club sport.

Being active is close to universal: 86% of adults did some sport or physical activity in 2025. Around seven in ten are active every week, half (50.1%) three times a week, but only about one in five every day.

People aged 55-64 are the most regular. At each step up to five times a week, they lead the next age group by 1 to 4 points. For daily activity, teenagers (24.3%) edge just ahead of them (23.2%).

People aged 65 and over rank last at most steps, yet 61.8% are still active weekly. From four to six times a week, they move ahead of adults aged 35-44.

Men and women are equally likely to be active weekly (68.9% and 68.8%).

Why this matters: Free time is shared with everything else in this story. The same hours can go to a club, a carer's role or a walk around the block.

Walking is the national favourite

Half of all adults walk for exercise: 11.4 million people. Fitness and gym work comes next with 7.7 million.

The five most popular activities, walking, gym, bushwalking, running and swimming, can all be done alone and on your own schedule. Team sport is a much smaller slice: football (soccer), the most-played team sport, has 1.3 million adult players.

Bushwalking alone outdraws all team sport. The five team sports in the top 20 (football, basketball, cricket, Australian football and netball) have 3.9 million adult participants between them, fewer than bushwalking's 4.0 million.

Studio classes are a large group of their own. Pilates (1.6 million) draws more adults than any team sport, and yoga (1.1 million) sits just behind football.

Racquet sports and golf together draw about 3.0 million adults, led by tennis (1.1 million) and golf (1.0 million).

Why this matters: Most adults stay active through footpaths, trails, pools and gyms rather than club competitions. Planning for physical activity needs to look beyond sports grounds to the everyday places where people walk, swim and train.

The ACT leads at every step

The Northern Territory is second at both levels, ahead of Western Australia and Victoria.

The gap from top to bottom is the same at both levels: 11.3 points. Weekly, it runs from the ACT down to South Australia (67.0%). At three times a week, it runs down to New South Wales (47.9%).

Weekly activity rose between 2024 and 2025 in seven of the eight states and territories. Western Australia gained the most (+4.0 points), followed by the Northern Territory (+3.1). Tasmania was the only one to slip, at once a week (69.6% to 69.4%) and at three times a week (53.3% to 51.3%).

Weekly activity is slightly higher in major cities (69.6%) than in regional and remote areas (65.8% to 67.0%), the reverse of the pattern for volunteering.

At three times a week, Western Australia (+4.2) and the Northern Territory (+4.0) also gained the most since 2024.

Even the lowest state is high: at least two in three adults were active weekly in every state and territory (67.0% to 78.3%).

Why this matters: The data shows where activity is higher, not why. Local factors such as facilities, climate and work patterns are worth a closer look.

Clubs add to activity, rarely replace it

Almost four in five adults (78.9%) do at least some informal activity. Just 42.0% do any organised activity.

Clubs and classes rarely stand alone. About 85% of people in organised activity also exercise informally (35.5% of adults out of 42.0%).

Organised activity fades with age. It reaches 59.8% of teenagers aged 15-17, but only 29.5% of people aged 65 and over.

Men are more likely to exercise only informally (45.3% against 41.6%), while women are slightly more likely to join organised activity (43.1% against 40.9%).

People aged 65 and over are the most likely to rely only on organised activity (7.6%), such as classes or clubs.

Informal-only activity almost doubles with age, from 26.6% of teenagers aged 15-17 to 51.8% of people aged 55-64.

Teenagers are the most likely to mix both kinds: more than half (53.1%) combine organised and informal activity.

Why this matters: Clubs reach younger people best, but most adults rely on time and places they choose themselves.

PART 4 - CONNECTION AND TAKEAWAY

Are active places also giving places?

Together, the data shows how volunteering, care and recreation shape community life differently across Australia.

Sport and volunteering do meet. One in three formal volunteers (33.3%) gives time to a sport or recreation organisation, more than any other kind of group.

But at the state level, being active does not predict giving time. Across the eight states and territories, the two measures are almost unrelated: the correlation is close to zero.

The eight places split evenly, two in each quarter of the chart. Only the Northern Territory and Western Australia sit above the middle on both measures.

The Northern Territory stands out across this story. It is above the national rate for all three kinds of giving, second for weekly activity and highest for childcare outside Darwin.

Why this matters: Each place has its own mix of habits. Support for volunteering and for activity needs to be planned separately, not assumed to rise together.

Volunteering

Fewer Australians volunteer than in 2010, but those who do gave more hours in 2025 than in 2020. Rates are highest in Tasmania, the Northern Territory and outside the major cities.

Care

2.5 million Australians are unpaid carers. Women carry more of it through most of adult life, and the Northern Territory outside Darwin stands out for childcare.

Activity

86% of adults are active, mostly on their own schedule. The ACT is the most active, but being active does not predict volunteering.

Everyday life in Australia runs on time freely given. Who gives it, and how, depends on where you live, how old you are and your gender.

Notes

Most figures are estimates from sample surveys (the General Social Survey and AusPlay), not counts. Small gaps between states, or between 2024 and 2025, may fall within sampling error. The ABS found no statistically significant change in national formal volunteering between 2020 and 2025.

The volunteering trend in chart 1 joins two survey series. The 2006 and 2010 rates cover people aged 18 and over; later years cover 15 and over, so part of the early fall reflects the change in who was counted.

Care figures are 2021 Census counts. The ABS adjusts small values to protect privacy, so regional totals can differ slightly from the national total of 2,476,681 carers. In chart 5, dots are spread within each region for illustration and do not show where people live.

AusPlay moved to an online survey in July 2023, so 2024 and 2025 results should not be compared with earlier years. People can take part in several activities, so the tiles in chart 10 add up to more than the adult population. The waffle squares in chart 12 are rounded to whole per cent so that they sum to 100.

Chart 13 compares the eight states and territories, not individual people. The correlation between the two measures, calculated for this story, is close to zero (r = 0.01). It describes places and does not show cause and effect.

Percentages are shown to one decimal place and may not add to 100 because of rounding. Every value can be checked against the project data files in the data folder.
